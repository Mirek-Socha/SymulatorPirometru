1. What is a pyrometer?

A pyrometer is a non-contact thermometer — it measures the temperature of an object without touching it, analyzing infrared radiation (IR) emitted by every body above absolute zero.

Every object "glows" with heat. The hotter — the more intensely and at shorter wavelengths. Glowing steel shines red because its IR waves are short. Your hand also emits IR — but at wavelengths too long for the eye to see.

Jak widzisz to na chartie?

The grey dashed curve (CDC — blackbody) is the maximum possible emission for a given temperature. The amber curve below is what the measured object actually emits — always weaker, depending on material. The violet curve is the signal reaching the detector after passing through the atmosphere.

Szara przerywana — wzorzec CDC (blackbody, ε = 1)
Amber — radiation emitowane przez object (ε·B)
Violeta — signal na detectorze (po atmosferze)
Cyan (dashed) — detector sensitivity R(λ)
Move the temperature slider and observe how the curves grow and shift to the left. Przy ok. 700°C object becomes visible to the naked eye as a red glow — Wien's law in action.

2. Emissivity — why does material matter?

Not every object emits the same amount of heat at the same temperature. Emissivity ε (from 0 to 1) describes how close an object is to an ideal blackbody (ε = 1).

Imagine a heated steel plate and a mirror — both at the same temperature. The mirror reflects heat instead of emitting it (ε ≈ 0.05). The plate emits heat efficiently (ε ≈ 0.7–0.9). Pyrometer skierowany na lusterko zobaczy prawie nic.

3. Atmosphere — why does distance matter?

The air between the pyrometer and the object absorbs some radiation — mainly water vapour (H₂O) and carbon dioxide (CO₂). The longer the optical path, the more radiation is lost along the way.

It is like looking through fog — the thicker and further, the less you see. Air is a "fog" for infrared, especially when humid or rich in CO₂ (e.g. near a furnace).

Efekt w praktyce

At a short path (2 m, low humidity) the atmosphere absorbs a few percent of signal — small error. At a long path (20 m, high humidity) the loss can be significant, especially at long wavelengths.

Try: select detector Far IR 8–14 µm, switch atmosphere to Long (20 m) and observe ΔT. Then switch to Near IR 0.65 µm at the same atmosphere — error much smaller, because short waves penetrate air better.

4. Environments — pyrometry beyond Earth

The simulator lets you explore what pyrometric measurement would look like in completely different atmospheres. Each environment changes gas composition and pressure — and thus transmittance τ(λ) and signal at the detector.

Imagine a pyrometer on a space mission. Would it work on Mars? On Venus? What about measuring welding temperature through a CO₂ shielding gas curtain? Switch to Expert Mode and find out — the answer is surprising!

Kluczowe obserwacje

Mars: Even though the air is nearly pure CO₂ (95%), pressure is 170 times lower than on Earth. The τ(λ) charts show the atmosphere surprisingly transparent — Beer-Lambert law counts the amount of gas, not its concentration.

Venus: Pressure 92 atm and temperature 465°C. The atmosphere itself emits so much radiation that the pyrometer "sees" mainly hot gas — not the object. ΔT is huge and always positive.

Underwater: Water absorbs infrared dramatically. After 10 cm there is no signal left at λ > 2 µm. The only option is the visible range — Si detector at 0.65 µm.

💡 Exercise: Go to Expert Mode → panel "🌐 Environment" → select Earth, Mars and Venus in turn at the same distance of 1 m. Observe how the τ(λ) chart and error ΔT change.

5. How to read the charts?

Chart spectrum radiation

The horizontal axis is wavelength λ (in µm). Shorter wavelengths are closer to visible light (0.4 µm). Longer wavelengths are deeper into infrared. The vertical axis is radiation intensity.

The vertical dashed cyan bar (R(λ)) shows the sensitivity range of the selected detector. The pyrometer "sees" only radiation in this narrow window.

Below the temperature reading you will find three circles:

Yellow ● — ΔT 5–30 K — check emissivity

1. Measurement channel model

A pyrometer measures temperature without contacting the object, by analyzing electromagnetic radiation. Signal at detector input includes six blocks of the chain and is the sum of three physical components fizycznych [5,8]:

$$\begin{aligned} L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda) = \tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)\cdot\Bigl[ &\underbrace{\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(\lambda,T)\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{obj}})}_{\text{object + atmosfera}} \\ &+ \underbrace{[1-\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{atm}})}_{\text{emisja atm.}}\Bigr] \\ &+ \underbrace{[1-\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{win}})}_{\text{emisja przes.}} \end{aligned}$$

τ_atm(λ) — atmospheric transmittance · τ_win(λ) — window transmittance · ε(λ,T) — emissivity objectu (grey model: const; model H-R: f(λ,T)) · background reflections omitted (T_bg ≈ T_atm)

The pyrometer processor assumes a simplified model: grey body with emissivity ε_assumed, no atmosphere or window (τ = 1). Mismatch with the actual signal generates error ΔT decomposed into three components (see section 8).

2. Prawo Plancka – blackbody

Spektralna luminancja energetyczna (radiance) blackbody (CDC) w temperature T [1,5]:

$$L_{bb}(\lambda,\, T) = \frac{2\,h\,c^{2}}{\lambda^{5}} \cdot \frac{1}{\exp\!\left(\dfrac{h c}{\lambda\, k_{B}\, T}\right) - 1} \qquad \left[\mathrm{W \cdot m^{-2} \cdot sr^{-1} \cdot m^{-1}}\right]$$

Uproszczenie Wiena (λ → 0)

For short waves (exp ≫ 1), i.e. when hc/λk_BT > 6, Planck's law is approximated:

$$L_{bb} \approx \frac{2\,h\,c^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\cdot e^{-c_{2}/\lambda T}, \qquad c_{2} = \frac{hc}{k_{B}} = 1{,}43878\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m{\cdot}K}$$
Full Planck law is used (without Wien approximation). Constants: h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 2.998×10⁸ m/s, k_B = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K.

Wien's displacement law

Wavelength of peak emission decreases inversely with temperature [2]:

$$\lambda_{\max}\cdot T = b = 2{,}8978\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m{\cdot}K}$$

Example: for T = 1200°C = 1473 K → λ_max = 1.97 µm (near IR), for T = 300 K → λ_max = 9.7 µm.

Prawo Stefana–Boltzmanna

Total CDC radiation flux (integral over all λ) [3]:

$$M = \sigma\, T^{4}, \qquad \sigma = 5{,}6704\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W{\cdot}m^{-2}{\cdot}K^{-4}}$$

3. Emissivity and Kirchhoff's Law

Emissivity ε(λ,T) defines the ratio of radiation emitted by a real body to CDC radiation w tej samej temperature [5,6]:

$$\varepsilon(\lambda,T) = \frac{L_{\mathrm{emit}}(\lambda,T)}{L_{bb}(\lambda,T)}, \qquad 0 \leq \varepsilon \leq 1$$

According to Kirchhoff's law [4]: ε(λ,T) = α(λ,T), where α is spectral absorptance — a body absorbing radiation at a given wavelength must emit it with the same intensity.

Default model is grey body (ε = const). Expert Mode offers additional models: Hagen-Rubens ε(λ,T) and polynomial TPRC.

Model Hagena-Rubensa — emissivity spektralna metali

For metals at long wavelengths (λ ≫ penetration depth) emissivity follows electron theory using Fresnel equations at high electrical conductivity [5,8]:

Using Fresnel equations at high electrical conductivity [5,8]:

$$\varepsilon(\lambda, T) \approx 0.365\sqrt{\frac{\rho(T)}{\lambda}} - 0.0667\frac{\rho(T)}{\lambda} + 0.006\left(\frac{\rho(T)}{\lambda}\right)^{3/2}$$

where ρ(T) [µΩ·cm] is electrical resistivity at temperature T, λ [µm]. Temperature dependence:

$$\rho(T) = \rho_0\left[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)\right], \quad T_0 = 293\,\mathrm{K}$$

Key properties of the H-R model:

  • ε decreases with λ (∝ 1/√λ for large λ) — metals are better emitters at short wavelengths
  • ε increases with T — because electrical resistivity of metals increases with temperature
  • Validity: λ > ~1 µm; at short wavelengths (VIS) Drude corrections are needed
Educational experiment: select material Fe (steel), set detector Si 0.65 µm and observe ε(0.65 µm, T) ≈ 0.35. Switch to MCT 8–14 µm — ε(10 µm, T) ≈ 0.12. Same object, same T, but pyrometer with ε_assumed = 0.70 makes different error in each range. This is exactly what justifies multi-spectral pyrometry.

Model wielomianowy — ε(λ) z danych literaturowych

For many materials spectral emissivity is well approximated by a second-order polynomial dopasowany do danych tabelarycznych z TPRC [A,B]:

Model is independent of temperature (ε = f(λ) only) — fit is valid in the stated range of λ and T. Outside the range result is extrapolated and may be less accurate.

Korekcja temperature (pyrometer monochromatyczny)

If the pyrometer reads brightness temperature T_J, the true temperature T_obj at emissivity ε is:

$$\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{obj}}} = \frac{1}{T_J} + \frac{\lambda}{c_2}\ln\varepsilon \qquad \text{(przyblizenie Wiena)}$$

Error for assumed wrong emissivity (ε_real ≠ ε_assumed):

$$\Delta T \approx -\frac{T^{2}\lambda}{c_{2}}\ln\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{real}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zal}}} \qquad \text{(przyblizenie Wiena)}$$

4. Atmospheric transmittance model

Atmospheric transmittance τ(λ) describes what fraction of radiation passes through an atmospheric layer of thickness L. Based on Beer-Lambert law [10]:

$$\tau(\lambda) = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{i} k_{i}(\lambda)\cdot c_{i}\cdot L\right), \qquad k_i\text{ -- wsp. absorpcji},\quad c_i\text{ -- stezenie}$$

W symulatorze stosujemy uproszczony model pasmowy oparty na danych HITRAN [9]:

The CO₂ band at 4.26 µm is so strong that 3–5 µm window pyrometers often use cut-off filters to avoid it. The 3.9 µm InSb window sits exactly between the 2.69 µm and 4.26 µm bands.

Self-emission atmosfery

The atmosphere, absorbing radiation, simultaneously emits it (Kirchhoff's law). Atmospheric emission is described by:

$$L_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda) = \bigl[1-\tau(\lambda)\bigr]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,\,T_{\mathrm{atm}}) \qquad \bigl(T_{\mathrm{atm}} = \text{const wzdluz toru}\bigr)$$

This emission is added to the object signal. It causes temperature overestimation (the pyrometer "sees" a warmer atmosphere).

5. Atmospheric environments — generalised model

The simulator models six different environments in which a pyrometer can operate. Each environment defines gas composition and pressure, which together determine spectral transmittance τ(λ) according to the generalised Beer-Lambert law:

$$\tau(\lambda) = \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \alpha_i(\lambda) \cdot c_i \cdot L \cdot \frac{P}{P_0}\right)$$

The P/P₀ factor (pressure relative to Earth) is crucial — the Martian atmosphere has 95% CO₂, but pressure 170× lower than terrestrial, so the effective column gas density is comparable to a few percent CO₂ on Earth.

🌍 Earth — model referencyjny

Uproszczony model pasmowy oparty na danych HITRAN [9]: 12 H₂O bands (0.72–13.4 µm), 7 CO₂ bands (dominant: 4.26 µm), O₃ band (9.6 µm). Trzy window pyrometeryczne: 0.8–1.3 µm, 3–5 µm, 8–14 µm.

🔴 Mars — optical density paradox

Composition: 95.3% CO₂, 2.7% N₂, 1.6% Ar, trace H₂O. Pressure: 0.006 atm (600 Pa).

$$\tau_{\mathrm{Mars}}(4.26\,\mu\mathrm{m}) \gg \tau_{\mathrm{Earth}}(4.26\,\mu\mathrm{m}) \quad \text{przy tym samym } L$$

Despite CO₂ concentration 2400× higher than on Earth, the 4.26 µm band on Mars is weaker for the same geometric distance. The result shows that the proper measure of absorption is kolumnowa amount gasu [mol/m²], nie concentration molowe.

Experiment: set L = 10 m and compare τ(4.26 µm) for Earth and Mars. Earth (400 ppm CO₂, 1 atm): τ ≈ 0.85. Mars (953 000 ppm CO₂, 0.006 atm): τ ≈ 0.94. The Martian atmosphere is more transparent — despite rich CO₂!

🟡 Venus — pyrometric inferno

Composition: 96.5% CO₂, 3.5% N₂, ~150 ppm SO₂. Pressure: 92 atm. T_atm = 465°C.

Two effects combine:

  1. Total absorption — CO₂ band at 4.26 µm opaque already after a few tens of centimetres. SO₂ bands at 4.0 and 7.3 µm block further ranges.
  2. Atmospheric self-emission — hot gas (465°C) emits intensely in the IR range. Signal at detector comes mainly from the atmosphere, not from the object.
$$L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda)\approx [1-\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{atm}}) \quad \text{(object nievisible)}$$
Pyrometric measurement on the Venusian surface is practically impossible by IR methods. Historically Venera probes used contact thermometers and radar.

🏭 CO₂ techniczny — welding MIG/MAG

MIG/MAG welding uses 100% CO₂ (or Ar+CO₂ mixtures) as shielding gas. At short paths (0.2–0.5 m) and pressure 1 atm the CO₂ band at 4.26 µm is completely opaque.

Pyrometers for monitoring welding pool temperature (1400–1700°C) must operate in the "window" between CO₂ bands. The industrial standard is InSb 3.9 µm — exactly in the gap between CO₂ bands at 2.69 µm and 4.26 µm.

6. Optical window — model kompletny

The optical window introduces three independent physical effects, modelled jointly by the simulator:

  1. Transmission attenuation τ_win(λ) — depends on material and thickness
  2. Self-emission — an absorbing window itself emits (Kirchhoff's law)
  3. Fresnel losses — surface reflections; included in τ_Fresnel (constant, independent of d)

Full channel equation with window

$$L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda) = \underbrace{\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)\cdot L_{\mathrm{po\ atm}}(\lambda)}_{\text{sygnal objectu przez window}} + \underbrace{[1-\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{win}})}_{\text{emisja wlasna window}}$$

Self-emission is analogous to atmospheric emission — a hot window (e.g. 300–500°C in a furnace wall) can bias the pyrometer reading upward, even when τ_win seems sufficient.

Transmittance model — Beer–Lambert with component breakdown

$$\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda,\, d) = \underbrace{\tau_{\mathrm{Fresnel}}}_{\text{stale}} \cdot \underbrace{S_{\mathrm{UV}}(\lambda)\cdot S_{\mathrm{IR}}(\lambda)}_{\text{odciecia pasmowe}} \cdot \underbrace{\tau_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\lambda,d_{\mathrm{ref}})^{\,d/d_{\mathrm{ref}}}}_{\text{absorpcja objetosciowa (Beer-Lambert)}}$$

Scaling with thickness — Beer–Lambert law

Volume absorption scales exponentially with thickness (Beer-Lambert law [10]). For a material with absorption τ_bulk(λ) at reference thickness d_ref:

$$\tau_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\lambda, d) = \tau_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\lambda, d_{\mathrm{ref}})^{\,d/d_{\mathrm{ref}}} = e^{-\alpha(\lambda)\cdot d}$$

Fresnel losses do not scale with thickness — they are a constant surface property. Therefore the model separates these two effects: doubling thickness amplifies band absorption but does not change reflection losses.

Example: 8 mm instead of 4 mm float glass. OH band at 1.9 µm with 9% absorption per mm contributes 72% vs 36% total attenuation. Fresnel losses (4%) remain unchanged.

Self-emission window

Kirchhoff's law: a body absorbing radiation must emit it. Window emissivity is:

$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda) = \alpha_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda) \approx 1 - \tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)$$

(reflections already included in τ_Fresnel). Signal emitted by the window:

$$L_{\mathrm{win,emit}}(\lambda) = [1-\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda, T_{\mathrm{win}})$$

For a cold window (T_win ≈ T_atm) the effect is negligible. For a hot window in a furnace wall (T_win = 400°C) self-emission can dominate in the 8–14 µm range and completely falsify MCT pyrometer measurements.

Tip for working with students: increase T_win to 400°C with the 8–14 µm detector and observe how the pyrometer reading rises despite T_obj being unchanged — pure window self-emission mimicking a hot object.

Parametry referencyjne materials

In practical pyrometric measurements the object is often observed through an optical window: a viewport in a furnace wall, protective glass, cryostat window or spectral filter. Each such element introduces additional attenuation described by spectral transmittance τwin(λ).

Model matematyczny

Signal at detector input accounting for the window:

$$L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda) = \tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda) \cdot \Bigl[\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)\cdot\varepsilon\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{obj}}) + [1-\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda)]\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T_{\mathrm{atm}})\Bigr]$$

The window does not emit radiation (assumption: Twin ≪ Tobj, typical for industrial measurements at high temperatures).

$$S = \int_0^\infty R(\lambda)\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda)\cdot L_{\mathrm{det,bez\,window}}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda$$

Implementation — band model with smoothing

Transmittance of each material is modelled by a function composed of smooth step edges (Hermite smoothstep) defining UV and IR cutoffs, and Gaussian absorption bands [7,10]:

$$\tau_{\mathrm{win}}(\lambda) = S_{\mathrm{UV}}(\lambda)\cdot S_{\mathrm{IR}}(\lambda)\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{peak}}\cdot\prod_k\!\left(1 - A_k\cdot e^{-(\lambda-\lambda_k)^2/2\sigma_k^2}\right)$$

The Hermite step function ensures continuous first derivatives at cutoff edges (no artefacts in spectral transmittance).

7. Modele funkcji odpowiedzi detectora R(λ)

Pyrometer pasmowy (gauss)

$$R(\lambda) = R_p\cdot\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda-\lambda_c}{\sigma}\right)^{\!2}\right], \qquad \sigma = \frac{\Delta\lambda}{2{,}355}$$

Used: Si (0.65 µm), InGaAs (1.0, 1.6 µm), InSb (3.9 µm). Narrow band minimises atmospheric absorption and emissivity dependence.

Window pyrometer (rectangle with softened edges)

$$R(\lambda) = \begin{cases} R_p & |\lambda-\lambda_c| < 0{,}88\,\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2} \\ 0 & |\lambda-\lambda_c| > \tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2} \end{cases}$$

Stosowany: InSb (3–5 µm), MCT/bolometr (8–14 µm).

Total-power pyrometer (broadband)

$$R(\lambda) \approx R_p = \mathrm{const}, \qquad \lambda \in [1{,}0\;\mu\text{m},\;17\;\mu\text{m}] \quad \text{(termostos, termopilek, bolometr)}$$

Measures total power. Very sensitive to emissivity, as there is no possibility to choose a "good" spectral window. Zgodny z prawem Stefana–Boltzmanna: M = ε·σ·T⁴.

Total-power pyrometer: \(T_{\mathrm{ind}} = \left(\dfrac{S}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zal}}\,\sigma}\right)^{1/4}\). Emissivity error proporcjonalny do \(T^4\) — bardzo wrażliwy!

8. Two-color pyrometry (stosunkowa)

Single-color pyrometry requires knowledge of emissivity ε — unknown for many materials in industrial conditions. Two-color pyrometry (ratio pyrometry) measures signal ratio at two wavelengths, eliminating ε for grey bodies.

Zasada fizyczna

Signaly przy λ₁ i λ₂ (uproszczone — bez atmosfery i window) [5,8]:

$$S_1 = \int R_1(\lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(\lambda)\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda$$ $$S_2 = \int R_2(\lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(\lambda)\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda$$

For a grey body ε(λ) = const → ε cancels from integrals and the ratio becomes:

$$\frac{S_1}{S_2} = \frac{\int R_1(\lambda)\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\int R_2(\lambda)\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda,T)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda} = f(T) \quad \text{(niezal. od } \varepsilon\text{!)}$$

Inversion: find Tratio such that the above ratio (at ε=1) equals the measured S₁/S₂. Algorytm: bisekcja 80 iteracji.

Wien approximation (narrow-band detectors)

For band pyrometers (Gaussian) with Wien condition satisfied (hc/λk_BT ≫ 1):

$$\frac{S_1}{S_2} \approx \frac{R_{\mathrm{p1}}}{R_{\mathrm{p2}}}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^5 \cdot \exp\!\left[\frac{c_2}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_2}-\frac{1}{\lambda_1}\right)\right]$$

Inwersja analityczna (szacunkowa):

$$T_{\mathrm{ratio}} \approx \frac{c_2\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda_2}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda_1}\right)}{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{S_1}{S_2}\cdot\dfrac{R_{\mathrm{p2}}}{R_{\mathrm{p1}}}\right) + 5\ln\!\left(\dfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)}$$

c₂ = 1.43878×10⁻² m·K; λ₁ < λ₂ (shorter/longer wavelengths). For grey body ε(λ₁)/ε(λ₂) = 1 → T_ratio is exact.

Gdy object nie jest szary, stosunek zawiera dodatkowy czynnik emissivity:

$$\frac{S_1}{S_2} = \frac{\varepsilon(\lambda_1)}{\varepsilon(\lambda_2)}\cdot f(T)$$

The processor ignores this factor and computes incorrect Tratio. Error:

$$\Delta T_{\mathrm{ratio}} \approx \frac{T^2}{c_2\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda_2}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda_1}\right)}\cdot\ln\!\frac{\varepsilon(\lambda_1)}{\varepsilon(\lambda_2)}$$
Experiment — Al₂O₃ (polynomial model): select pair Si 0.65 / InGaAs 1.0 µm. Przy Al₂O₃ (a₁ > 0) ε(0.65 µm) ≈ 0.28 i ε(1.0 µm) ≈ 0.50 → ε₁/ε₂ ≈ 0.56. Procesor ratio "sees" too small a ratio and underestimates Tratio. For graphite (near grey) ΔTratio ≈ 0.

Applicability conditions and limitations

  • Assumes grey body — for selective materials (glass, ceramics, oxides) error can be large; T_ratio may be worse than single-color.
  • Requires identical channel conditions — atmosphere and window must have identical τ at both λ; this is rarely satisfied (e.g. CO₂ band at 4.26 µm)
  • Reduced T sensitivity when Δ(1/λ) is small — close-wavelength pairs have lower temperature resolution
  • S₁/S₂ ratio requires calibration of both channels — asymmetric gain drifts create systematic error independent of temperature.
In the simulator the ratio processor assumes τ_atm = τ_win = 1 at both λ. In a real channel atmospheric absorption at λ₁ ≠ absorption at λ₂, which introduces additional error (especially near the CO₂ band at 4.26 µm in the 3–5 µm range). The full channel model with atmosphere and window works correctly — the S₁/S₂ ratio computed from real signals accounts for τ(λ).

9. Signal integration and temperature inversion

Signal detectora

Total electrical signal (proportional to power) at the detector output:

$$S = \int_{0}^{\infty} R(\lambda)\cdot L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda$$

where L_det(λ) is the full channel equation (see section 1). In the simulator: N = 700 points, λ ∈ [0.10, 16] µm, integration by rectangle method (step DL = (LAM_MAX − LAM_MIN)/N).

$$S \approx \sum_{i=0}^{N} R(\lambda_i)\cdot L_{\mathrm{det}}(\lambda_i)\cdot\Delta\lambda$$

Inwersja temperature — algorytm bisekcji

The pyrometer processor assumes a simplified model (grey body with emissivity ε_assumed, no atmosphere and window) and finds T_ind satisfying [5,8]:

$$\text{find}\;T_{\mathrm{ind}}:\quad\sum_{i=0}^{N} R(\lambda_i)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zal}}\cdot L_{bb}(\lambda_i,\,T_{\mathrm{ind}})\cdot\Delta\lambda \;=\; S$$

Algorithm: bisection, 72 iterations, range T ∈ [1, 15 000] K, convergence < 0.01 K. Computational complexity: ~72 × (N+1) ≈ 50 700 Planck law evaluations per slider movement.

Note on ε model in inversion: processor always uses ε_assumed = const, even if the Hagen-Rubens model is selected. The inconsistency ε(λ) ≠ ε_assumed is intentional — exactly how a real pyrometer works, which does not know the true spectral emissivity of the object. Ten error jest visible w componentsj ΔT_ε.

Error decomposition — 3 independent components

Total error ΔT = T_ind − T_obj is decomposed into three orthogonal components, determined by progressive "unveiling" of the channel:

$$\Delta T_{\varepsilon} = T_{\mathrm{ind}}\!\left[\varepsilon(\lambda,T),\;\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}\!=\!1,\;\tau_{\mathrm{win}}\!=\!1\right] - T_{\mathrm{obj}}$$ $$\Delta T_{\mathrm{atm}} = T_{\mathrm{ind}}\!\left[\varepsilon(\lambda,T),\;\tau_{\mathrm{atm}}(\lambda),\;\tau_{\mathrm{win}}\!=\!1\right] - T_{\mathrm{ind}}\!\left[\varepsilon,\;\tau\!=\!1,\;\tau\!=\!1\right]$$ $$\Delta T_{\mathrm{win}} = T_{\mathrm{ind}}\!\left[\text{pelny tor}\right] - T_{\mathrm{ind}}\!\left[\varepsilon,\;\tau_{\mathrm{atm}},\;\tau_{\mathrm{win}}\!=\!1\right]$$ $$\Delta T = \Delta T_{\varepsilon} + \Delta T_{\mathrm{atm}} + \Delta T_{\mathrm{win}}$$
